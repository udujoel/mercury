# Architecture Decisions

## ADR-001 — Server generates, phone plays

**Status:** accepted

iOS `BGAppRefreshTask` grants roughly 30 seconds of CPU. Generation takes two
to four minutes. Background windows are also opportunistic, granted based on
usage patterns, battery, thermals and network, with no guarantee.

So: all ingestion, clustering, LLM and TTS work runs server side on a cron. The
finished audio and manifest are pushed to the device by silent notification.
The iOS app is a player with local caching.

Rejected alternative: on-device generation with Apple Foundation Models. Fails
on time budget and on battery, and TTS quality on-device is not competitive.

## ADR-002 — Clean dialogue in the script, naturalness from the TTS model

**Status:** accepted

The NotebookLM team confirmed that the micro-interjections making their audio
sound human are generated by the audio model, not written into the transcript.

So the LLM writes clean dialogue. If output sounds robotic we change the TTS
provider. We never add filler words to the prompt — it makes output worse.

## ADR-003 — Cluster stories, do not summarise articles

**Status:** accepted

Taken from Particle. Without clustering you hear the same story five times
because five newsletters covered it. Clustering is the difference between a
digest that feels edited and one that feels concatenated.

At personal scale (hundreds of items per day) scikit-learn agglomerative
clustering over an in-memory matrix is sufficient. No vector database until
the app serves more than one user.

**Status note, 2026-08-31.** The decision has not changed. What changed is
that it now runs: from Phase 2 until this date the clustering code had no
caller outside its own tests, so nothing in a generated briefing had ever
been deduplicated. Newsletters and articles are clustered in a single pass
(one `build_stories` call over `emails + articles`) rather than separately,
which is the whole reason both normalise into `Item` first. Still untested
against live feeds: the 0.78 threshold is tuned for Gemini embeddings and
has only ever been exercised offline.

## ADR-004 — Every AI provider behind a protocol

**Status:** accepted

Free tiers get deprecated and pricing changes. LLM, TTS and embeddings each sit
behind a `Protocol` in `app/core/providers.py`. Swapping a provider means one
new file, never a pipeline change.

## ADR-005 — AVAudioEngine over AVPlayer

**Status:** accepted

Transcript sync displays one large sentence at a time. AVPlayer position
reporting is not precise enough to keep that in sync with speech.
AVAudioEngine gives sample-accurate frame positions.

Cost: we implement seek, rate control and Now Playing integration ourselves.
Worth it — the transcript is the visual composition of the player screen.

## ADR-006 — Google Neural2 now, ElevenLabs next; music is part of the product

**Status:** accepted

The Phase 0 gate passed. Real Google Cloud TTS Neural2 output cleared the ten
second test — Joel's verdict was "the audio is good, but can be better", which
is a pass, not an endorsement.

So Neural2 stays for now and ElevenLabs is the planned upgrade rather than an
emergency swap. That is exactly the case ADR-004 exists for: `TTSProvider` in
`app/core/providers.py` is already satisfied by `GoogleTTSProvider`, so the
migration is one new file in `app/providers/` plus an env change, with no
pipeline edit.

What changes with ElevenLabs, and why it is worth the roughly 22 USD a month:
prosody and disfluency are native to the model, which is precisely the quality
CLAUDE.md rule 1 says we buy from the TTS engine rather than write into the
script. Flash v2.5 is also sub-300ms per turn, which matters for the Phase 3
voice interrupt where the whole latency budget is 2.5 seconds.

**Intro and outro music.** A briefing that starts cold on a voice sounds like a
file playing. A short stinger before the cold open and under the sign-off is
what makes it sound like a show, and it is the cheapest identity the product
can have. Constraints: a few seconds only, ducked well under the voice, and the
timing manifest must account for its duration or every transcript offset shifts
by the length of the intro. That last point is the real engineering content
here — `synthesize_briefing` currently assumes turn zero starts at 0ms.

Rejected: generating music per briefing. It is slow, expensive, non-deterministic
and nobody wants a different theme every morning. Two fixed audio files, chosen
once.

## ADR-007 — Free provisioning is the target; APNs is optional, not required

**Status:** accepted

Joel does not intend to buy an Apple Developer Program membership. That is a
constraint on the design, not a gap in it.

Exactly one capability is genuinely blocked: **APNs remote push**, which needs
the Push Notifications entitlement. Everything the product actually depends on
works on a free personal team — on-device `SFSpeechRecognizer`, microphone,
background audio, `BGProcessingTask`, and local notifications.

An earlier note in this project claimed the paid account also blocked the
Phase 3 voice gate. It does not. Speech recognition needs no entitlement; it
needs a physical device, which free signing provides. Getting that wrong would
have pushed a working feature into a "blocked" bucket for no reason.

So the three-layer delivery strategy in ADR-001 keeps its shape, minus its top
layer:

    PRIMARY    server cron -> APNs silent push      unavailable when free
    SECONDARY  BGProcessingTask prefetch            works
    FALLBACK   foreground fetch on launch           works

and `PushService` arms a repeating **local** notification at the wake time
whenever APNs registration fails, carrying the same "Play" action (PRD F-17).
The APNs code stays: it is already written and tested behind a `PushProvider`
protocol, and it starts working the day a membership exists without a rewrite.

What is lost: the wake notification fires on the device clock rather than when
the server actually finished. Since generation runs hours before the wake time,
that difference is usually invisible.

What is deferred rather than solved: TestFlight distribution (Phase 5) needs a
membership, and the 7-day provisioning expiry means re-running from Xcode
weekly. Neither blocks daily personal use.

Rejected: dropping the APNs code. It costs nothing to keep, it is the correct
architecture per ADR-001, and deleting working tested code to match a temporary
account tier would have to be undone later.

## ADR-008 — GitHub Actions generates, Cloudflare R2 serves, no hosted backend

**Status:** accepted

A cloudflared quick tunnel from a laptop was the delivery mechanism through
Phases 1-3. It is fine for a demo and wrong as infrastructure: it dies when the
Mac sleeps, the hostname changes on every restart, and it enforces a hard
120-second proxy read timeout that a 127-second generation cannot clear.

Surveying free backends made the real problem obvious: **Mercury is not one
service, it is two workloads with opposite requirements**, and hosting them
together is why every free tier looked wrong.

    generation   127s, once a day, idle 23h58m    -> wants a scheduled runner
    serving      a few-KB manifest, a ~4MB file   -> wants object storage
    voice (Join) sub-2.5s round trip               -> wants a warm process

So:

- **GitHub Actions cron** runs generation daily. A private repo gets 2000
  minutes a month; a two-minute daily job uses about 60. `apt install ffmpeg`
  works, and secrets live in repository settings.
- **Cloudflare R2** stores the audio and manifest. 10GB free with **zero
  egress fees**, which matters because the phone downloads ~4MB every morning.
- **The phone reads directly from R2.** No backend to host, no cold start, and
  nothing that depends on a laptop being awake.

**Voice is deferred, not deleted.** It is the only piece needing a warm
process, and every free tier's 30-60 second cold start exceeds the entire 2.5
second budget by an order of magnitude. It is PRD priority P2 — below data
connections, the pipeline, playback and automation — so it is the right thing
to drop from the hosted path. The code is built, tested and merged; it keeps
working whenever the Mac is running, and it costs nothing to leave in place. If
it ever becomes daily-use important, roughly 5 USD a month on a small VPS runs
everything in one place.

Rejected:

- **Bonto** — Node.js only, explicitly no Python. The entire backend is Python.
- **Koyeb** — acquired by Mistral AI in February 2026; no new free signups.
- **Render free** — the trap on every list. Python and Docker both supported,
  but it spins down after 15 minutes, so the 05:00 cron never fires and every
  card tap waits 30-60 seconds.
- **Vercel / Netlify synchronous functions** — 10 second timeout against a
  127 second job.
- **Railway / Fly.io** — no meaningful permanent free tier in 2026.

**Dependency this exposes:** the Google OAuth consent screen is in Testing
mode, where refresh tokens expire after seven days. Unattended daily
generation will stop every week wherever it runs. Publishing the app to
Production removes the expiry and needs no verification review for a single
user on `gmail.readonly`.

## ADR-009 — Voice (the Join flow) is dropped

**Status:** accepted, supersedes the Phase 3 portion of the roadmap

Joel's verdict: "a nice to have but rarely ever used."

Voice was PRD priority **P2** — the lowest band, below data connections, the
content pipeline, playback and automation. It was built, reviewed and merged
in full (Phase 3, commit c701e4f), and it worked: the server side measured
1642-1977ms to first audio against a 2500ms budget.

It is removed anyway, for two reasons that compound:

1. **It is the only workload that needs a warm server.** Every free tier's
   30-60 second cold start exceeds the entire 2.5 second budget by an order of
   magnitude. Keeping voice meant paying for hosting; dropping it made the
   fully free architecture in ADR-008 possible.
2. **It was not free to keep in the app.** It required microphone and speech
   recognition permission prompts for a feature that would rarely be used, and
   PRD 3.4 gave the mic the largest touch target on the player screen.

What was removed: `app/pipeline/voice.py`, `app/api/voice.py`, the iOS
`Core/Voice/` module, `JoinOverlay`, `VoiceAskClient`, the mic control, both
Info.plist permission strings, and the associated tests.

What was kept: `PlaybackCoordinator.duck`/`restoreVolume`. They are small,
tested, and audio ducking is generally useful.

**Recovering it** is `git show c701e4f` — the full implementation, its tests
and its two reports are in history. This is a decision about what ships, not a
judgement that the work was wrong.

## ADR-010 — Cloudflare triggers the morning run; GitHub's schedule is a backstop

**Status:** accepted

The product promise is that the briefing is finished before the user wakes up.
It was not. Seven consecutive days on `cron: "0 4 * * *"` (04:00 UTC):

    2026-08-23  04:32   success   ~32 min late
    2026-08-24  04:40   success   ~40 min late
    2026-08-25  04:34   success
    2026-08-26  04:35   success
    2026-08-27  14:57   FAILURE   ~11 hours late
    2026-08-28  15:52   success   ~12 hours late
    2026-08-29  10:42   success   ~7 hours late

Three consecutive days the briefing was generated in the afternoon, for a
product whose only job is to be ready at 07:00. `createdAt == startedAt` in
every one of them, so this is not a queue draining — GitHub's scheduler
*created* the run that late.

### What GitHub actually guarantees

Nothing, and the documentation says so plainly:

- "The `schedule` event can be delayed during periods of high loads of GitHub
  Actions workflow runs. High load times include the start of every hour."
- "If the load is sufficiently high enough, some queued jobs may be dropped."
- "To decrease the chance of delay, schedule your workflow to run at a
  different time of the hour."

`cron: "0 4 * * *"` was therefore the single worst minute available. The top of
the hour is the congested window GitHub names, and we had picked it.

(Also documented, and not our cause but worth knowing before this repo is ever
made public: "In a public repository, scheduled workflows are automatically
disabled when no repository activity has occurred in 60 days." Mercury's repo
is private, so the schedule does not expire from inactivity today.)

### Do more cron entries fix it?

Partly, and not enough. This option deserved to be taken seriously: it is free,
adds no dependency, and generation is already idempotent, so a later duplicate
run costs one R2 read and exits 0.

The question is whether several entries are independent draws. **They are
not.** The delay GitHub documents is a function of global Actions load at a
point in time, not a property of the individual cron expression — which is
precisely why the mitigation offered is "run at a different time of the hour"
rather than "add more entries". Two entries five minutes apart therefore sample
almost the same load and fail together. Two entries hours apart are independent
only to the degree the load at those hours is. That is enough to fix the 32-40
minute top-of-hour cases, and it is not enough for the three multi-hour days,
which would plausibly have swallowed every entry inside one morning window.

Dropping is the harder half. A dropped run leaves **no run record at all** — no
failed run, no red tick, no failure email, nothing to find except an absence.
More entries lower the probability that every one is dropped. They cannot bound
it, and they cannot bound the delay either, because no bound exists to rely on.

So multiple entries are a real improvement that costs nothing, and they cannot
meet a hard deadline alone. They are kept, demoted to a backstop, and moved off
the hour.

### The decision

The deadline is: **the briefing is in R2 before 06:00 Europe/Lisbon.** Lisbon is
UTC+1 in summer, so the tight case is 05:00 UTC and everything is sized against
it. Cron is UTC-only with no DST on both platforms, and the briefing is still
NAMED in the user's timezone by `app.cli` — this project has had five separate
date bugs and that is the one that matters.

**The deadline comparison is UTC, not local, and in winter it is an hour
early.** In winter 06:00 Europe/Lisbon *is* 06:00 UTC, so a fixed 05:00 UTC
deadline means the alarm can fire up to 52 minutes before the deadline it names.
That is a deliberate choice in the safe direction, and the alternative does not
fit: a DST-correct comparison needs an alarm checkpoint after 06:00 UTC for
winter AND one before it for summer, and Cloudflare's free plan allows **five
Cron Triggers per account**, which the four generation checkpoints plus one
alarm already exhaust. Given the choice between alarming early in winter and
alarming after the user has woken in summer, early wins: by the alarm
checkpoint four generation attempts have failed and the last has had half an
hour, so it is an early alarm about a real absence, not a false one.

Three layers, each covering the layer above it failing:

1. **A Cloudflare Cron Trigger is the punctual trigger.** The Worker already
   exists (ADR-008) and already holds the R2 binding. Cloudflare documents
   that "Cron Triggers execute on UTC time" and documents no delay-or-drop
   caveat comparable to GitHub's. Four checkpoints — 03:40, 04:20, 04:50,
   05:08 UTC — each of which asks R2 whether a playable briefing exists and
   calls `workflow_dispatch` only when the answer is no.

2. **GitHub's own `schedule` is the backstop**, at 04:12 and 04:41 UTC. Off the
   hour, and deliberately *after* the Worker's 03:40 primary: the earliest
   successful run decides how fresh the briefing is, so an earlier backstop
   would win the race on a healthy day and hand the user a staler inbox for
   nothing. These exist for the Worker being broken.

3. **A watchdog that goes red.** `python -m app.cli check` asks storage whether
   today's briefing is there and playable, and `briefing-watchdog.yml` fails
   when it is not — which converts the absence into the failure email the user
   already gets. The watchdog also has its own (late, best-effort) schedule for
   the case where the Worker is the thing that died.

   **The alarm gets its own checkpoint, at 05:40 UTC, and dispatches nothing
   else.** It was originally dispatched in the same tick as the 05:08
   generation. `workflow_dispatch` only QUEUES a run, so the watchdog read R2
   before the run it had just triggered had written anything, and reported a
   missing briefing about a briefing being generated as it looked. A watchdog
   that cries wolf every morning is worse than no watchdog: the email it sends
   is the only signal this design has, and it stops being read the moment it is
   usually wrong. 32 minutes is more than ten times the observed 71-second
   generation, plus Actions queueing and checkout. The watchdog additionally
   re-checks twice, 120 seconds apart, before failing, and GitHub's own first
   backstop moved from 05:27 to 05:52 — 19 minutes after the 05:08 dispatch
   was not margin, and the Actions load that eats 19 minutes is the condition
   this watchdog exists to tolerate.

Two details that carry more weight than their size:

- **The Worker's check needs no credential.** The R2 binding is already there.
  The alarm therefore keeps working when the GitHub token is the thing that
  broke — otherwise the watchdog would fail in exactly the situation it exists
  for.
- **An empty briefing is dispatched with `force: true`.** Idempotency means a
  stored manifest, even one with zero turns, makes every later run decline to
  replace it. Without this the self-healing path cannot heal the one failure
  that most needs it.

### What this costs, honestly

A **GitHub fine-grained personal access token, stored as a Worker secret** —
scoped to this repository alone, with the Actions permission and nothing else.
That is new attack surface on a Worker that previously held only a read token,
and it is the real price of this ADR.

Weighing it:

- The token is used only from `scheduled()`, which is **not reachable over
  HTTP** — no request to the Worker can invoke it. The exposure is a bug in the
  `fetch` handler that leaks `env`, which is the same exposure the existing
  `MERCURY_API_TOKEN` already has.
- It cannot read code, read secrets, or push. It can start workflows.
- Blast radius if it leaks: an attacker can dispatch `daily briefing`
  repeatedly with `force: true`, which serialises on the existing concurrency
  group and burns Actions minutes plus real Gemini and TTS spend. That is a
  cost attack, not a data breach, and it is bounded by the 2000-minute monthly
  allowance. It is still the thing to revoke first if anything looks wrong.
- It introduces a **second credential that expires silently**, alongside the
  Google OAuth token ADR-008 already flagged. This is the part that genuinely
  argues against the design. The mitigation is layer 2: if the token dies, the
  GitHub backstop still generates and the watchdog still alarms, so the failure
  degrades to "punctuality lost" rather than "briefing lost".

**Rejected:**

- **Multiple `cron:` entries alone.** Analysed above. Kept as a backstop,
  rejected as the answer, because no documented bound on delay or dropping
  means no way to meet a deadline.
- **Moving generation to Cloudflare.** Workers cannot run the Python pipeline,
  ffmpeg, or a 127-second job. This is ADR-008's reasoning and it has not
  changed.
- **Generating far earlier (e.g. 01:00 UTC) to buy slack.** Free and
  dependency-free, but it pays in the currency the product is actually made of:
  every hour earlier is an hour of inbox and calendar the briefing does not
  know about.
- **A launchd timer on the Mac.** Punctual and free, and precisely the laptop
  dependency ADR-008 removed.
- **A third-party cron service** (cron-job.org and similar). Same PAT exposure
  as the Worker, on infrastructure we do not control, for a job the Worker can
  already do.

## ADR-011 — 30-day retention, and an admission about the 7-day one

**Status:** accepted

Nothing deleted old briefings. Each morning writes roughly 3MB of audio plus a
manifest and nothing ever removed either, against a 10GB free tier. At that
rate the tier lasts about nine years, so this was never urgent — it was
unbounded, which is a different and quieter problem.

**The part worth recording is that we already believed this was solved.**
`r2_storage.py` opened with "a 7-day retention of ~4MB briefings uses about
30MB of it", and `docs/DEPLOYMENT.md` said the same thing in its cost table.
There was no 7-day retention. Not in the code, not in the workflow, not in a
bucket lifecycle rule, not anywhere. Two documents described a policy nobody
had implemented, and both read as reassurance every time anyone checked
whether storage was handled.

That is this project's own recurring failure mode — the same shape as the
degraded-briefing warning that keyed off a signal which structurally always
existed, and the same shape as inferring that R2 reads worked because R2
writes did. So it is written down here rather than quietly corrected in a
docstring: **the documentation was wrong, for months, in the reassuring
direction.**

**The decision:** `prune_briefings` in `backend/app/synthesis/storage.py`, 30 days by
default via `RETENTION_DAYS`, run as a step of the daily workflow.

30 rather than 7 because the constraint was never really storage — 30 days is
about 90MB — and being able to go back and listen to something from three
weeks ago is worth more than 99.1% of the free tier versus 99.7%.

The design is dominated by one risk: this is code whose job is deletion,
running unattended every morning, next to the object the user is about to
listen to. So:

- **Two overlapping guards on today's briefing.** The arithmetic already keeps
  it (a window of at least one day cannot reach today), and there is an
  explicit `>= today_id` check as well. The second guard costs one string
  comparison, and "the retention job deleted this morning's briefing" is not a
  bug worth discovering in production.
- **`RETENTION_DAYS=0` is refused, not obeyed.** A zero-day window is a
  misconfiguration; reading it as "delete everything including today" would be
  technically consistent and catastrophic.
- **Anything not positively identifiable as a briefing is left alone.**
  Retention matches `YYYY-MM-DD.` at the start of a key and ignores the rest.
- **Future-dated briefings are kept.** `--briefing-id` accepts any date, so
  they can exist, and they are not old whatever the subtraction says.
- **It never fails the morning.** One undeletable object is logged and skipped,
  and the workflow step is `continue-on-error`. Housekeeping must not be the
  reason a briefing run reports failure.

Listing is keyed off object names rather than manifests, so audio orphaned by a
run that died between writing the audio and writing the manifest is still found
and still reclaimed — that audio is ~3MB of the ~3MB.

**Rejected: an R2 bucket lifecycle rule.** It is the obvious answer, it is free,
and Cloudflare supports it. Two reasons against. It cannot express "never
delete today" — lifecycle rules work on object age, and a briefing regenerated
with `--force` gets a fresh timestamp while an old one that was never replaced
keeps its original, so age and briefing date are not the same thing. And it is
configuration that lives only in a dashboard, which is precisely how the
imaginary 7-day retention survived for months: nobody could tell by reading the
repository whether it existed. This way it is in the code, it is in the
workflow, and it is tested.

## ADR-012 — Particle's light editorial register, superseding PRD 3.3 and 3.4

**Status:** accepted

The visual register moves from Huxe's ambient dark to Particle's light
editorial. Warm off-white paper, a serif for anything a host says, SF for
anything the app says, generous margins, and colour carried by ink rather than
by atmosphere.

**Huxe still governs behaviour.** One tap to the thing you came for, playback
that survives the screen locking, and no dashboard to manage. That is the part
of Huxe worth keeping and none of it is affected here. What changes is only
what the app looks like while doing it.

### What this supersedes

PRD 3.3 and 3.4, by name, in full.

PRD 3.3 opens: *"This is the Huxe home screen pattern, reconstructed from the
app screenshots. A pure black canvas holding a deck of large rounded cards,
each one a data source with its own ambient colour identity."* Every clause in
that sentence is now false. The canvas is `#FBFAF8`. There is no deck. Cards do
not have individual colour identities — the edition has one colour and every
surface is a variation on it.

PRD 3.4 specifies three vertical zones with *"the currently spoken sentence
rendered as large centred white text"* over a full-bleed ambient background,
and argues: *"The text is the visual composition, which is what makes the
screen feel designed rather than functional."*

**That argument is right, and it survives.** It is the best sentence in either
section, and it is why the player works at all: the sentence is not a caption
under something else, it is the thing on screen. What changes is the execution,
not the intent. On paper a large serif line set ragged-right against a wide
margin is also a composition — the way a printed pull-quote is a composition —
and it composes better than centring does, because centred text on a light
ground has no left edge for the eye to return to and reads as a greetings card
rather than as something someone is saying to you. Centring earns its place in
an ambient dark player, where the text floats in a field with no other
structure to align to. It stops earning it the moment there is a margin.

So: the sentence stays the composition, stays large, stays the only thing in
the top zone, and keeps its 220ms cross-fade. It is set ragged-right in the
serif instead of centred in SF.

### One documentation correction that came out of this

CLAUDE.md's first paragraph cited `docs/PRD.md`. That file does not exist and
appears never to have. The real specification is
`docs/mercury_ios_prd_blueprint.docx`. Source and test files carry 53 explicit
`PRD <section>` citations across 12 distinct sections — count them with
`grep -rhoE "PRD [0-9]+\.[0-9]+|PRD F-[0-9]+" ios/ backend/app scripts worker/src`
— every one of which points a reader at a path that is not there.

That number was 47 when this ADR was written and is 53 now: merging #13 into
this branch brought six more citations in with the news pipeline. A count in
prose goes stale on someone else's commit, which is worth stating in an ADR
whose whole complaint is documentation that reads as authoritative and is not
checkable. The command is the durable part; the number is a snapshot, and if it
disagrees with the command the command wins. (`worker/src` is in the path list
for completeness and currently contributes none of them.)

Every section number in those citations was checked against the docx and every
one is real — PRD 3.3, 3.4, 3.6 and 3.7 all say what the code claims they say.
So this was a wrong path, not invented provenance. Worth recording anyway,
because a standing brief whose first paragraph points at a missing file is the
same drift that produced the imaginary 7-day retention in ADR-011: a document
that reads as authoritative and cannot be checked without effort. CLAUDE.md now
points at the real filename.

### The real cost: the card deck does not survive

PRD 3.3's fanned deck — cards offset vertically and horizontally, rearmost most
inset, brought forward on vertical drag with `matchedGeometryEffect` — is a
dark-register device. It works because the cards are luminous objects floating
in black. On paper, overlapping white cards on off-white have almost no
luminance difference to separate them, so the fan reads as a stack of
misaligned paper rather than as depth, and the drag interaction becomes a
gesture you have to discover in order to see content that could simply have
been listed.

`CardDeck.swift` is therefore deleted in task 2 rather than reworked, and Home
becomes a vertical list. This also permanently settles the "deck sizing is off"
complaint, which was a symptom of trying to make a 0.62-screen-width fanned
card work across device sizes, not a tuning problem.

That is a real loss. The deck is the most distinctive thing in the current app
and the thing most obviously borrowed from Huxe. The judgement is that it was
distinctive in a register we are leaving, and that keeping it would make the
new register incoherent rather than making the deck work.

### Why the structural work is the token layer

The interesting part of this change is not choosing colours. It is that the app
was *incapable* of light mode: every colour was a hardcoded literal —
`Color.black` for the canvas, `Color.white.opacity(0.72)` for secondary text —
so there was nowhere to put a second value. There was no seam.

`AdaptiveColor` holds both appearances as data, which is what makes any of the
rest possible, and has a second benefit that turned out to matter more: a token
whose value is data can be *measured*. `MercuryColor.textTertiary` shipped at
`white.opacity(0.45)`, which is 4.41:1 on black and fails WCAG AA for body
text, and nothing in the codebase said so either way because a `Color` cannot
be asked what it is. Contrast is now computed in tests, in both appearances,
against every ground a token can land on. That found two further failures
during this work that eyes had passed over — including one where a token
cleared AA on bare paper and fell under it once the ambient tint was drawn
underneath.

The weekday accent idea survives the register change intact, but its constraint
gets much harder: the seven anchors were background fills that only had to sit
*under* white text, and are now foreground ink that has to clear 4.5:1 *on*
`#FBFAF8`. They are pinned by relative luminance rather than by HSB brightness,
because equal brightness is not equal lightness and a hue-jittered accent at
fixed brightness drifts in and out of AA depending on which hue it lands on.

`AmbientBackground`'s per-process `Hasher()` seed is replaced with FNV-1a in
the same pass — see the file's own comment for the measurements. Its docstring
promised "same title always produces the same visual identity" and that held
only until you killed the app.

## ADR-013 — The digest is a sibling of the manifest, and the desk is bigger than the edit

**Status:** accepted

Joel asked to "view all newsletters that dropped, and delve into the stories
in them". PR #13 made that answerable for the first time: RSS, embeddings,
clustering and ranking had been built and never called, so `Story` objects
with real cross-source members did not exist at generation time until then.
This decides what is persisted for that screen and where.

### The audio is the edit; the digest is the full desk

`LENGTH_BUDGETS` gives the hosts four stories at standard length. A real
morning clusters far more than four — 201 articles and 54 emails became 222
stories on 2026-09-03 — and every one of the other 218 was scored, ranked and
dropped on the floor inside `_build_world_stories`, unreachable by anything.

So a story with `covered_in_audio: false` is the normal case, not a defect.
The difference between what was said and what arrived is the product; a
digest carrying only what reached the audio is the manifest's source list
under another name.

### A sibling file, not a bigger manifest

`{briefing_id}.digest.json` beside `{briefing_id}.manifest.json`, served at
`GET /briefing/{id}/digest` behind the same token.

The manifest is what the player downloads to start playing audio. The digest
is ~100KB on a busy morning. Folding one into the other would make every
launch pay for a screen the user may never open, on a phone, before the audio
starts.

### `turn_index` is read off the manifest, and that is the whole correctness argument

Every covered story deep-links into the audio: tap a story, hear the hosts
discuss it. `turn_index` therefore has to index the manifest's
**post-compaction** `turns` array — `synthesize_briefing` drops turns whose
TTS failed (two of forty-three on a live run) and `_remap_source_refs`
rebases every `SourceRef` onto the shortened list.

`build_digest` is handed the finished manifest and reads `manifest.sources`.
There is no second index to get wrong because it never sees the
pre-compaction numbering at all. This project has already shipped that
off-by-one once, which is why the guarantee is structural rather than
careful.

The join needed one new field: `SourceRef.cluster_id`. The alternative was
matching on `(title, source_name)`, and two items can share both — a feed
publishing "Morning Briefing" daily, carried in a window spanning two of them
— which puts them in different clusters and deep-links a story into another
story's moment.

### Rule 5 holds structurally, not by care

What persists is derived: LLM-written headlines and summaries, plus metadata
(`title`, `source_name`, `url`, timestamp) that already persists today in
`SourceRef`. **No extractive snippets** — an extract is the raw body with a
smaller word count.

Bodies are read once, to build the enrichment prompt, before
`_scrub_email_bodies` runs, exactly as `script_chain` already reads them to
write the script. `build_digest` reads no body at all, so it produces a
byte-identical artifact before and after the scrub, and that is asserted.

The privacy policy at joeludu.com/mercury was updated in the same change. It
listed exhaustively what persists, and a fourth item now exists; the page is
public and Google read it to publish the consent screen.

### Enrichment is bounded at 40 stories; the desk is not

The interface contract asked for an LLM headline and summary on every story.
222 summaries in one call is roughly 15k output tokens — slow enough to
threaten the PRD's 90-second budget, deep enough into a long structured
emission to reinvite the malformed-JSON failure that lost the 2026-08-27
briefing, and a real draw on the same free-tier quota the four script passes
spend.

So every story still appears, and only the re-writing is capped. An unenriched
story carries the publication's own headline — real, human-written, and
already persisted in `SourceRef` — and a null summary. The fallback is
explicitly not a snippet.

Which stories get the budget is not "top N by score". Newsletter-backed
clusters always qualify, however they ranked: they are the feature that was
asked for, and a pure score rule would spend the budget on wire copy while
the newsletter the ranker dropped — precisely the one the user opened the app
to find — got a bare subject line.

"Always" stops at 40, though, or the bound is not one. Review caught that the
priority was applied BEFORE the cap, so a morning with 41 newsletter-backed
clusters sent every one of them into a single call — the unbounded emission
this whole section exists to prevent, on a day the inbox is busiest. A live
run ingests 54 emails, so that is a Tuesday. The slice is taken off the
score-sorted list, so the cap costs the lowest-ranked newsletters their
summary and nothing their place on the desk.

The call runs concurrently with TTS, so it costs no wall clock against a
71-second live run, and it is cancelled when synthesis raises rather than
left holding the loop open for a briefing that no longer exists.

### Personal email is not a story, and not part of one either

A cluster reaches the digest only if it contains a newsletter or an RSS
article. An ordinary message from a colleague is neither, and rendering it as
a browsable "story" would put private correspondence on a screen built for
published journalism and hand it to an LLM to summarise on top of that.

That test alone was not enough, which review found and this ADR originally
got wrong. Clusters are MIXED: emails and articles go into one
`build_stories` call, which is the whole reason the `Item` schema is shared,
so a colleague writing about a story the wire also carried is in the same
cluster as the article. The cluster passed on the article's strength and
every member was then read — so the personal message's sender, subject and
timestamp were serialised into `sources[]`, and in a newsletter-backed
cluster (where every member is `EMAIL`, so `authority_sorted` falls through
to "whoever reported it first") that message could be the canonical whose
body went to the model and whose subject line became the headline.

So the rule is per MEMBER, not per cluster: `digest_members` is the single
filter every read of a cluster goes through — `sources[]`, the item the
summariser gets, the headline fallback, the source names in the prompt, and
which refs may deep-link the story. That last one is the subtle case:
`_build_source_refs` gives a personal email a ref carrying its cluster id, so
without the filter the inbox turn reading out that colleague's message marked
the newsletter's story `covered_in_audio` and became its deep-link. Cluster-
level coverage is still right — a turn about the Reuters report covers the
story three newsletters also wrote about — but only the members the screen
shows may vote for it.

### Degradation

- A failed enrichment pass costs the summaries, never the briefing and never
  the browse surface (rule 7 applied to a screen).
- A digest that will not write is logged and the briefing still publishes.
  The manifest plus the audio is the briefing; a secondary object must not be
  able to withhold it. (Only `R2Storage` did this at first; the local backend
  raised, so a full disk cost the briefing rather than the screen.)
- **The stored digest was built from the stored manifest, or there is none.**
  `--force` reuses the briefing id, so a digest surviving from an earlier run
  for the same day carries `turn_index` values addressing THAT run's turn
  list: a browse screen whose taps land in audio the bucket no longer holds,
  wrong in the way that looks like it worked. Both backends therefore
  withdraw the previous digest whenever a new one is not published — a failed
  write, or no digest this run — and withdraw the new one if the manifest
  then fails to land. If the previous one cannot be removed the run FAILS
  rather than publishing over it, on the same argument the stale-audio branch
  in `R2Storage.save` already makes: the old manifest and the old digest are
  a consistent pair, so the user keeps the briefing they already had.
- The local digest is written through a temporary file and renamed.
  `write_text` truncates first, and the API serves the same directory the
  generator writes to, so a regeneration made the digest read as absent for
  as long as the write took.
- A missing digest is an ordinary 404 on both read paths. Every briefing
  generated before this existed has none, so the client shows an empty state,
  and the audio is unaffected.
- `schema_version: 1` from day one, so a future shape can be refused by an
  older client rather than silently rendered as an empty list — a screen that
  says nothing and looks like "no newsletters today".

**Rejected:**

- **Extending `BriefingManifest`.** Above: every player launch pays for it.
- **An extractive snippet as the summary fallback.** It is the raw body with a
  smaller word count, which is the thing rule 5 forbids. Null is honest.
- **Summarising from titles alone, to avoid sending bodies to the model.** A
  summary written without the source is the model guessing what an article
  probably said, which is a rule 3 failure dressed as a privacy win. The
  bodies already go to Gemini to write the script; the digest adds no
  recipient.
- **Capping how many stories appear.** That is the one thing the feature is
  for. Bounding the enrichment costs a summary; bounding the desk costs the
  answer to the question that was asked.

## ADR-014 — Jev judges what goes in the inbox segment; the heuristic is the floor

**Status:** accepted

Email triage moves from `classify_priority` — four lines of keyword and
header matching — to a TypeSafe Jev System One judgment. The heuristic is
not deleted. It keeps running at ingestion and its verdict stands wherever a
judgment does not arrive.

### Why the heuristic had to go

Not because the rules were badly chosen. Because *"does this matter to Joel
this morning"* is a semantic question, and every patch to a keyword
approximation of a semantic question fights an earlier patch.

The file says so itself. `is_urgent` exists, ahead of the newsletter floor,
because a bank's account-restriction warning was pinned LOW — `List-Unsubscribe`
made it a newsletter, and it sorted below a gym mailer by arrival time (#55).
`"?" in body` marks any marketing "Ready to upgrade?" as a question aimed at
the user. `_is_an_order`, the same technique one module over, was found by
review of #57 to miss 24 of 28 realistic phrasings and to catch "confirm your
payment" while missing "verify your payment".

### Three judgments, not one label

The root fault in `classify_priority` is arity. It returns one tuple from one
rule chain, so it cannot say *bulk **and** serious* — which is precisely what
the bank alert is. The fix had to be an ordering between branches.

Jev is asked three independent questions over the same state, in one request:

| | primitive | why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `consequence` | `Score`, 4 levels | a rankable position, so ties break on what matters rather than on arrival time |
| `awaiting_reply` | `Noul` | maps onto `Item.requires_response`, which `"?" in body` was guessing |
| `bulk` | `Noul` | replaces the `List-Unsubscribe` check, and is free to disagree with `consequence` |

Held separately the contradiction does not arise and no branch needs to be
ordered ahead of another.

### The scale is a level index, not a fraction

`Score` returns a position on the criteria index. With four levels it runs
0.0–3.0. Measured against real mail on 2026-09-21:

```
bank account-restriction warning   consequence 3.00  awaiting 0.23  bulk 0.57
colleague's dated request          consequence 1.99  awaiting 0.86  bulk 0.05
"Ready to upgrade?" marketing      consequence 0.00  awaiting 0.12  bulk 0.85
```

The first draft thresholded HIGH at 2.5, having assumed a normalised 0–1
scale. That demotes every *a person is waiting* email — they land at ~2.0 —
to MEDIUM. The cut is 1.5. No fixture would have caught this, because a
fixture written under the wrong assumption agrees with itself.

### Degradation

Per-item. A judgment that fails leaves that email carrying the heuristic's
verdict and a null `consequence`, which sorts it by recency within its tier —
the pre-ADR behaviour, for that one item. A pass that fails entirely, or a
missing `TYPESAFE_API_KEY`, leaves every email that way, which is the
pre-ADR behaviour exactly.

A provider returning the wrong NUMBER of judgments is the one case that is
refused outright rather than degraded: zipping a short list onto the items
hands each email its neighbour's verdict, and a briefing wrong in a way
nobody can see is worse than one wrong in a way the log names.

### Rule 5

`app/pipeline/triage.py::email_state` is the only place an `Item` becomes a
payload, and it sends a fixed allowlist — sender, subject, recipients,
`List-Unsubscribe` presence, timestamp, and the first 500 characters of the
cleaned body. An allowlist rather than a redaction list so a field added to
`Item` later cannot silently start leaving the machine.

This is a second vendor seeing inbox content. Gemini already does, so it is a
difference of degree rather than of kind, and it is stated here rather than
left to be discovered in a diff.
